# Textos de Tomás

* [ST I, q.1, a.1 — Whether, besides philosophy, any further doctrine is required](st-i-q1-a1.md) - Aquinas argues that a revealed doctrine is necessary because man is directed to God as to an end that surpasses the grasp of reason.
* [ST I, q.1, a.8 — Whether sacred doctrine is a matter of argument](st-i-q1-a8.md) - Sacred doctrine argues from the articles of faith as its principles and can answer objections against them.
* [ST I, q.2, a.1 — Whether the existence of God is self-evident](st-i-q2-a1.md) - A proposition can be self-evident in itself but not to us.
* [ST I, q.2, a.2 — Whether it can be demonstrated that God exists](st-i-q2-a2.md) - There are two kinds of demonstration: from the cause (propter quid) and from the effect (quia).
* [ST I, q.2, a.3 — Whether God exists (the five ways)](st-i-q2-a3.md) - The five ways proceed from motion, efficient causality, contingency, gradation of perfection, and the governance of things.
* [ST I, q.3, a.4 — Whether essence and existence are the same in God](st-i-q3-a4.md) - In God essence and existence (esse) are identical: God does not have being, he is his own act of being.
* [ST I, q.3, a.7 — Whether God is altogether simple](st-i-q3-a7.md) - God is absolutely simple: he is not composed of parts, of matter and form, of essence and existence, of genus and difference, or of substance and accidents.
* [ST I, q.5, a.1 — Whether goodness differs really from being](st-i-q5-a1.md) - Goodness and being are the same in reality and differ only in idea.
* [ST I, q.12, a.1 — Whether any created intellect can see the essence of God](st-i-q12-a1.md) - The created intellect can see God's essence, for otherwise the natural desire to know the cause of what we see would be in vain and our ultimate happiness would be impossible.
* [ST I, q.12, a.12 — Whether God can be known in this life by natural reason](st-i-q12-a12.md) - Our natural knowledge begins from the senses, so reason can know of God only what can be known from sensible effects: that he exists, that he is the first cause of all things, and that he differs from all things he…
* [ST I, q.13, a.5 — Whether what is said of God and of creatures is univocally predicated of them](st-i-q13-a5.md) - Names such as 'good' or 'wise' are said of God and creatures neither univocally nor purely equivocally, but analogically, according to proportion: the perfection exists in God primarily and in creatures by participation.
* [ST I, q.14, a.1 — Whether there is knowledge in God](st-i-q14-a1.md) - Knowing beings differ from non-knowing beings in that they can possess the form of another thing as well as their own.
* [ST I, q.16, a.1 — Whether truth resides only in the intellect](st-i-q16-a1.md) - Truth is found primarily in the intellect and secondarily in things, insofar as they are related to an intellect.
* [ST I, q.19, a.8 — Whether the will of God imposes necessity on the things willed](st-i-q19-a8.md) - Because the divine will is supremely efficacious, not only do the things God wills come about, but they come about in the way he wills: some necessarily and some contingently.
* [ST I, q.20, a.2 — Whether God loves all things](st-i-q20-a2.md) - God loves all existing things, since to love is to will good to someone and God's will is the cause of all goodness in things.
* [ST I, q.22, a.2 — Whether everything is subject to the providence of God](st-i-q22-a2.md) - All things, even individual and contingent events, are subject to divine providence, because God's causality extends to everything that has being.
* [ST I, q.25, a.3 — Whether God is omnipotent](st-i-q25-a3.md) - God can do all things that are possible absolutely, that is, whatever does not imply a contradiction.
* [ST I, q.29, a.1 — The definition of person](st-i-q29-a1.md) - Aquinas accepts Boethius's definition of person as an individual substance of a rational nature.
* [ST I, q.44, a.1 — Whether it is necessary that every being be created by God](st-i-q44-a1.md) - Whatever is found in something by participation must be caused by that to which it belongs essentially.
* [ST I, q.46, a.2 — Whether it is an article of faith that the world began](st-i-q46-a2.md) - That the world had a beginning in time is held by faith alone and cannot be demonstrated, just as its eternity cannot be demonstrated.
* [ST I, q.48, a.1 — Whether evil is a nature](st-i-q48-a1.md) - Evil is not a being, form or nature, but the absence of a good that ought to be present: a privation.
* [ST I, q.75, a.1 — Whether the soul is a body](st-i-q75-a1.md) - The soul is the first principle of life in living things.
* [ST I, q.75, a.2 — Whether the human soul is something subsistent](st-i-q75-a2.md) - The intellectual principle has an operation of its own in which the body does not share, namely understanding, which can know the natures of all bodies.
* [ST I, q.75, a.4 — Whether the soul is man](st-i-q75-a4.md) - Man is not his soul alone but a composite of soul and body, because sensing, which belongs to man, is not an operation of the soul alone.
* [ST I, q.75, a.6 — Whether the human soul is incorruptible](st-i-q75-a6.md) - The human soul is incorruptible.
* [ST I, q.76, a.1 — Whether the intellectual principle is united to the body as its form](st-i-q76-a1.md) - The intellectual principle, which we call mind or intellect, is united to the body as its substantial form.
* [ST I, q.76, a.2 — Whether the intellectual principle is multiplied according to the number of bodies](st-i-q76-a2.md) - Against the Averroist thesis of one intellect for all men, Aquinas argues that each human being has his own intellect: it is impossible for one intellect to belong to many men, since then this man would not…
* [ST I, q.79, a.1 — Whether the intellect is a power of the soul](st-i-q79-a1.md) - The intellect is a power of the soul and not the very essence of the soul.
* [ST I, q.79, a.3 — Whether there is an agent intellect](st-i-q79-a3.md) - Because the natures of material things are only potentially intelligible, there must be a power in the soul that makes them actually intelligible by abstracting them from material conditions: the agent intellect.
* [ST I, q.79, a.12 — Whether synderesis is a special power of the soul distinct from the others](st-i-q79-a12.md) - Synderesis is not a power but a natural habit by which we grasp the first principles of practical reason, as the habit of first principles grasps those of speculative reason.
* [ST I, q.79, a.13 — Whether conscience is a power](st-i-q79-a13.md) - Conscience is not a power but an act: the application of knowledge to a particular action, by which we judge that something should be done or not done, or has been done well or badly.
* [ST I, q.82, a.1 — Whether the will desires something of necessity](st-i-q82-a1.md) - The will necessarily adheres to the last end, happiness, as the intellect necessarily adheres to first principles.
* [ST I, q.83, a.1 — Whether man has free will](st-i-q83-a1.md) - Man has free will because he acts from judgment that is not natural instinct but a comparison made by reason.
* [ST I, q.84, a.6 — Whether intellectual knowledge is derived from sensible things](st-i-q84-a6.md) - Intellectual knowledge takes its beginning from the senses.
* [ST I, q.84, a.7 — Whether the intellect can understand without turning to phantasms](st-i-q84-a7.md) - In the present life the intellect cannot actually understand anything without turning to sense images (conversio ad phantasmata).
* [ST I, q.85, a.2 — Whether the intelligible species abstracted from phantasms are related to our intellect as that which is understood](st-i-q85-a2.md) - The intelligible species is not what is understood but that by which the intellect understands (id quo, not id quod).
* [ST I, q.93, a.4 — Whether the image of God is found in every man](st-i-q93-a4.md) - Man is in the image of God chiefly by his intellectual nature, capable of knowing and loving God.
* [ST I, q.96, a.4 — Whether in the state of innocence man would have been master over man](st-i-q96-a4.md) - Political authority is natural and not merely a consequence of sin.
* [ST I-II, q.1, a.1 — Whether it belongs to man to act for an end](st-i-ii-q1-a1.md) - Properly human acts are those that proceed from deliberate will, and the object of the will is the end and the good.
* [ST I-II, q.1, a.4 — Whether there is a last end of human life](st-i-ii-q1-a4.md) - There cannot be an infinite series of ends, since then nothing would be desired and no action would begin or terminate.
* [ST I-II, q.2, a.8 — Whether any created good constitutes man's happiness](st-i-ii-q2-a8.md) - After showing that happiness consists neither in wealth, honor, fame, power, bodily goods nor pleasure, Aquinas concludes that no created good can constitute happiness.
* [ST I-II, q.3, a.8 — Whether man's happiness consists in the vision of the divine essence](st-i-ii-q3-a8.md) - Final and perfect happiness can consist only in the vision of the divine essence.
* [ST I-II, q.5, a.3 — Whether one can be happy in this life](st-i-ii-q5-a3.md) - A certain participation in happiness can be had in this life, but perfect and true happiness cannot, because this life is subject to unavoidable evils and the good desired cannot be possessed without end.
* [ST I-II, q.5, a.5 — Whether man can attain happiness by his natural powers](st-i-ii-q5-a5.md) - Imperfect happiness can be acquired by natural powers, in the way virtue is acquired.
* [ST I-II, q.6, a.1 — Whether there is anything voluntary in human acts](st-i-ii-q6-a1.md) - The voluntary is what proceeds from an intrinsic principle together with knowledge of the end.
* [ST I-II, q.18, a.4 — Whether the goodness or malice of a human action depends on the end](st-i-ii-q18-a4.md) - A human act has a fourfold goodness: generic, as an action;
* [ST I-II, q.19, a.5 — Whether the will is evil when it is at variance with erring reason](st-i-ii-q19-a5.md) - Every conscience, whether right or erring, binds: whoever acts against the judgment of his reason, even if mistaken, has an evil will, because he wills what he apprehends as evil.
* [ST I-II, q.19, a.6 — Whether the will is good when it abides by erring reason](st-i-ii-q19-a6.md) - Following an erring conscience excuses from sin only if the error is involuntary and not culpable.
* [ST I-II, q.26, a.4 — Whether love is properly divided into love of friendship and love of concupiscence](st-i-ii-q26-a4.md) - To love is to will good to someone.
* [ST I-II, q.55, a.4 — Whether virtue is suitably defined](st-i-ii-q55-a4.md) - Aquinas defends the definition of virtue as a good quality of the mind by which we live righteously, of which no one can make bad use.
* [ST I-II, q.57, a.4 — Whether prudence is a distinct virtue from art](st-i-ii-q57-a4.md) - Art is right reason about things to be made (recta ratio factibilium);
* [ST I-II, q.61, a.2 — Whether there are four cardinal virtues](st-i-ii-q61-a2.md) - There are four cardinal virtues: prudence perfects reason itself;

* [ST I-II, q.64, a.1 — Whether moral virtues observe the mean](st-i-ii-q64-a1.md) - Moral virtue consists in a mean between excess and defect, measured by right reason.
* [ST I-II, q.65, a.1 — Whether the moral virtues are connected with one another](st-i-ii-q65-a1.md) - Perfect moral virtues are connected, because none can exist without prudence, and prudence cannot exist without the moral virtues that rightly dispose us toward ends.
